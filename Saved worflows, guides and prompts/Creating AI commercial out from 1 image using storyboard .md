# 🧠 The Core Idea Behind This Workflow

The biggest difference in this workflow is the use of a **storyboard generation system**.

!Notion template 2.png

Instead of asking Seedance to randomly invent a video from a single image, we first create a structured storyboard containing multiple cinematic frames.

Those frames become the visual foundation for the final advertisement.

This gives dramatically more control over:

- storytelling
- shot progression
- pacing
- visual consistency
- camera logic
- cinematic flow

**Seedance 2.0 animation prompt:**

(Generated by automated system prompt inside the workflow)

```java
Here is a polished storyboard-generation prompt:

Create a 12-frame storyboard contact sheet for a short product advertisement, designed as a clean visual overview for video development.

Use the uploaded product image as the exact product reference. Preserve the product's shape, packaging, label design, colors, logo placement, proportions, materials, finish, and key branding details consistently in every frame where it appears. Do not redesign, simplify, rename, recolor, or invent new label elements.

Use the uploaded style reference image as the visual-style guide. Apply its mood, framing logic, lighting language, color treatment, texture, camera feel, polish level, and overall ad aesthetic across the storyboard. Do not copy unrelated objects, people, products, logos, text, locations, or scene content from the style reference unless explicitly requested.

Brand concept: The product is a wireless headphone designed for agency-grade cinematic visuals, with a focus on raw street style. The main character is a ginger woman wearing a black and red outfit, including a loose red t-shirt jacket.

Visual style: The visual language should be cinematic, with a focus on wide shots, medium shots, close-ups, and extreme close-ups. The camera feel should be dynamic, with a mix of handheld and static shots. The lighting language should be natural, with a focus on available light. The color treatment should be bold, with a focus on reds and blacks. The texture should be gritty, with a focus on urban environments. The camera feel should be fast-paced, with a mix of quick cuts and slow-motion shots. The polish level should be high, with a focus on clean visuals and minimal noise.

Video intent: The storyboard should feel like a real commercial sequence designed for a future video edit. Each panel should be composed as a believable 16:9 widescreen shot, with a focus on cinematic framing and composition. The sequence should be readable left to right, then top to bottom, without needing visible numbers.

Storyboard format: Generate one clean 12-panel storyboard contact sheet presented as a horizontal visual overview. Arrange the panels in a clear contact-sheet layout with consistent spacing and subtle visual separation. Do not include frame numbers, captions, arrows, callouts, annotation marks, written shot labels, or other visual clutter.

Panel aspect-ratio rule: Every individual panel within the storyboard must itself be framed as a distinct 16:9 widescreen shot. This requirement applies to each scene, not just to the outer canvas. Each panel should clearly appear as a cinematic widescreen frame suitable for video.

Sequence logic: The storyboard should communicate progression through layout, action, screen direction, subject movement, product interaction, and visual continuity rather than through numbering or written labels. The viewer should understand the order of the sequence from the grid layout and the natural progression of the images alone.

Story arc: The overall mini-story should be a strong product hero shot, with a focus on the ginger woman wearing the black and red outfit and loose red t-shirt jacket. The sequence should build towards a final resolving frame that leaves the product, benefit, or brand world clearly memorable.

Frame-by-frame direction:

01 — Establishing shot of the ginger woman walking down a city street, with the product visible in her hand.
02 — Close-up of the product, with the ginger woman's hand holding it.
03 — Medium shot of the ginger woman wearing the product, with a focus on the product's design and features.
04 — Wide shot of the ginger woman walking down a city street, with the product visible in her hand.
05 — Close-up of the product, with the ginger woman's hand holding it.
06 — Medium shot of the ginger woman wearing the product, with a focus on the product's design and features.
07 — Wide shot of the ginger woman walking down a city street, with the product visible in her hand.
08 — Close-up of the product, with the ginger woman's hand holding it.
09 — Medium shot of the ginger woman wearing the product, with a focus on the product's design and features.
10 — Wide shot of the ginger woman walking down a city street, with the product visible in her hand.
11 — Close-up of the product, with the ginger woman's hand holding it.
12 — Final resolving frame of the ginger woman wearing the product, with a focus on the product's design and features.

Composition rules: Vary shot size, lens feel, angle, and camera perspective throughout the sequence. Keep the product visible often enough to support branding, but integrate it naturally into the story unless the concept specifically calls for heavy product focus. Make the frame list feel intentionally directed, not generic. Think like a director planning a short ad: each frame should suggest a useful shot for a future edit.

Final-frame rule: The last frame should feel like a deliberate commercial closing shot, not just another scene. It should visually resolve the story and leave the product, benefit, or brand world clearly memorable. The final frame does not always need to be a traditional product packshot. Depending on the concept, the closing frame may be a strong product hero shot, a use-case payoff shot, an emotional payoff shot, a brand-world closing shot, or an end-card-style composition. Choose the closing type that best fits the concept, but ensure it feels final, intentional, and commercially effective.

Frame-writing guidelines: Write each frame as a concise but vivid visual instruction. Make each frame distinct in function, composition, and camera perspective. Avoid repetitive phrasing across frames. Preserve any strong scene ideas from the user, but rewrite them into cleaner and more precise visual directions. Translate the style reference into practical wording rather than vague aesthetic labels. Keep the wording efficient, specific, and visually actionable. Favor video-friendly descriptions: action, movement, screen direction, camera distance, lens feel, subject placement, and product interaction.

Output rules: Output only the final polished prompt. Do not mention these internal instructions. Do not include placeholders unless the user truly provided too little information to avoid them. Do not ask follow-up questions. If necessary, make reasonable creative decisions and complete the prompt in a way that remains faithful to the rough idea, product image, and style reference.
```
